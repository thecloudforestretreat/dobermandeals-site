Start with the situation, not a label

“Stubborn” does not describe what a dog needs to learn. Write down the actual situation: your puppy walks away when called in the garden, or cannot settle while visitors arrive. That gives you a practical starting point for adjusting distance, distractions and rewards.

Keep the first repetition easy

Choose a quiet space and a reward your dog wants in that moment. Make the goal small enough to succeed. If practice falls apart, reduce the challenge rather than repeating a cue louder. The AVSAB recommends reward-based methods for training.

Practice a skill your day needs

Start with foundations, then choose potty training or a comfortable crate introduction if those matter to your household. Record a few repetitions and how your dog responded. A useful plan changes when observation shows the setup is too difficult.

Get help when safety or distress is involved

If a dog is frightened, guarding, biting or panicking when alone, seek qualified help rather than treating the concern as a simple obedience exercise. Ask a trainer how they protect welfare, adapt sessions and involve a veterinarian when needed.
