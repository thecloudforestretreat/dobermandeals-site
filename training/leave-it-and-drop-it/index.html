Leave it and drop it: two different Doberman skills

Leave it means move away before taking something; drop it means release something already held. Teach each separately with safe practice items and rewarding exchanges.

By Doberman Deals · Updated October 12, 2026

Choose safe practice before choosing a cue

Use a quiet space, ordinary rewards and a toy your dog comfortably releases. Keep medication, food wrappers and hazardous objects out of reach rather than using them as tests. Do not involve children in exchanges or practice with an item your dog guards.

Teach moving away first

For leave-it practice, make a low-value item inaccessible without confronting your dog. Reward a voluntary turn away with a different reward from your other hand. Keep repetitions brief. Add the verbal cue when turning away is predictable, then introduce easier real-world distractions with access safely managed.

Make dropping a fair exchange

Offer a familiar safe toy, then offer a reward your dog wants without reaching into their mouth. Mark and reward the voluntary release. Return the toy when safe so letting go does not always end enjoyment. Add your drop cue as the release becomes predictable; avoid chasing or prying.

Compare two practice moments

An example: your dog looks at a safe toy on a shelf, turns toward you and receives a reward elsewhere—that is leaving it. Later, your dog releases a toy during a relaxed exchange—that is dropping it. Record these separately so success at one does not conceal difficulty with the other.

Protect safety when practice breaks down

Freezing, growling or a tense grip calls for distance and professional help, not a better test. Manage access to items and contact a qualified reward-based professional. If ingestion of a dangerous item is suspected, contact a veterinarian promptly; trained cues do not replace urgent care or physical prevention.

Common questions

Should I pull an item from my dog’s mouth?

Do not use forced removal as a training exercise. Guarding needs a safe management plan and professional support; suspected dangerous ingestion needs veterinary advice.

Does leave it guarantee safety outdoors?

No. Manage access, supervise and use appropriate equipment. Training is an additional skill, not a guarantee.
