Loose-leash walking with your Doberman

Teach a comfortable walking pattern in an easy setting. Use safe equipment and short practice periods before adding busy surroundings.

By Doberman Deals · Updated October 12, 2026

Check fit and choose your starting space

Inspect your leash, attachment points and walking equipment before practice. Fit should allow comfortable movement without slipping or rubbing. Start in a safe, quiet area where you can change direction without traffic or crowded paths. Seek help with equipment fit if you are unsure.

Define the walking goal

For this guide, the goal is a comfortable leash with slack, not a formal heel position. Decide which side and reward location you will use consistently. Begin with your dog nearby and reward the position you want before expecting a long stretch of walking.

Practice a few easy steps

Take a small number of steps and notice when the leash remains loose. Deliver a suitable reward near your chosen position. Pause or return to an easier setup when your dog moves too far ahead. Avoid jerking the leash or using corrections to force the dog into place.

Separate practice from a difficult outing

A crowded street adds demands beyond the first exercise. Choose quieter times or another route while the skill develops. Keep necessary outings manageable and avoid expecting every minute outdoors to be a training test. If pulling creates a safety risk, arrange professional help rather than choosing a harsher tool.

Use your log to adjust

Record the location, distractions, reward and what helped maintain slack. Change one feature at a time: a little distance, another quiet setting or a mild distraction. If your dog struggles, reduce the challenge. Good walking is a shared practical skill, not a guarantee of control in every situation.

Common questions

Is loose-leash walking the same as heel?

No. A practical loose-leash walk allows comfortable movement without continuous tension. Formal heelwork is a different task.

What if the route is too busy?

Choose a quieter time or place and separate skill practice from necessary outings. Ask for help if you cannot manage safely.
