Doberman health-testing records: understand what they show

Match each result to the right dog and test date. Genetic results, clinical screening and a veterinary examination provide different kinds of information.

By Doberman Deals · Updated October 12, 2026

Start with identity and the original report

Collect the dog’s registered name or identifier, test name, date and issuing organization. Match those details before comparing results. A screenshot with a cropped name or missing date is a reason to request the complete record, not enough information to reach a health conclusion.

Separate genetic information from clinical assessment

A genetic test addresses the variants included in that test. A clinical evaluation examines particular findings at a particular time. A general examination has another scope. Ask a veterinarian what each report means for the individual dog; one category does not replace all the others.

Understand the CHIC number

OFA explains that a CHIC number indicates completion of the breed’s required screening and public availability of results. It does not mean every result was normal. Open the individual records and check the findings rather than using the number alone as a health rating.

Verify the current breed requirements

Use the current OFA CHIC requirements and breed-club resources, then ask for help interpreting the actual reports. Requirements and clinical recommendations can change. Avoid copying an old checklist as a complete current screening plan or inventing follow-up intervals from a seller’s summary.

An example: build a records index

Create one line per report: dog identifier, test, date, source and question for the veterinarian. If a record is absent or unclear, mark it “not verified” in your private notes and request clarification. This organizational example does not classify a dog as suitable for breeding or rule out disease.

Common questions

Does a CHIC number mean all tests were normal?

No. Review the individual public results and ask about their meaning and limitations.

Does a DNA panel replace clinical screening?

It does not provide the same information. Ask which assessments are relevant to the individual dog.
